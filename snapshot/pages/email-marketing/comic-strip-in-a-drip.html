i snuck a strip into an e-mail drip

  • E-mail Marketing
  • Copywriting
  • Design

i wrote a lot of e-mails at Plane. sixty-something, across seven drips. i planned them, wrote them, and designed them. every one was an onboarding e-mail, which means it was the first thing a new customer heard from us after signing up.

the tricky and fun part was that our customers weren’t one group of people, they almost never are. someone on a trial wants something different from someone who’s already paying, and both want something different from someone on the free cloud plan who hasn’t decided if they care yet. so each group got its own drip.

e-mails to trial users

the trial e-mails were the ones i lost sleep over and my fav ones. most people treat trial e-mails like the opening act, the band nobody paid to see, playing to a half-empty room while everyone waits for the paying customers to walk in. i think that's backwards. a trial user is your ideal customer standing in the doorway with one foot already outside, and you don't give that person a tour of the building. you give them a reason to stay. so i didn't write product tours. i gave each e-mail one use case and one question: what does this actually fix for the person reading it? then i made it fun, and every e-mail became a small case study told as a comic strip. i love every e-mail i've written like it's one of my kids, but these are the ones i let run wild. i experimented more here than anywhere else, and the open rates went up. read for yourself :)

e-mails to free users

the free cloud e-mails were a different kind of hard. and they were the firsttt task i was assigned when i joined Plane. a free user isn't standing in the doorway, they're sitting on the porch. they signed up, they like the view, and they have no plans to come inside, because nothing is asking them to. so i stopped trying to sell them the house. every e-mail in this drip did one small thing: it showed them something they already had and weren't using, the kind of feature you stumble on two months in and think, wait, this was here the whole time? it was. it always is. the goal was never to get them to pay. it was to get them to open Plane on a random tuesday for no reason at all, because the people who use something on a random tuesday are the ones who eventually reach for their card without being asked.

e-mails sent to paid customers

and then there were the people who'd already said yes. the sale is closed, the confetti's swept up, and the e-mails turn into receipts and release notes. but a paying customer is the easiest person in the world to lose, because they're the only one actually keeping count. every month the invoice lands and they ask themselves, quietly, is this still worth it? so these e-mails had one job: to answer that question before they ever had to ask it. not with a changelog. with the workflow the team two desks over was already using, the shortcut that gives them back an hour on a friday. i wrote them like notes from a friend who works there and knows where all the good stuff is hidden. less selling, more showing them around the house they already bought.

fan mail (unsolicited)

IMPACT

i wrote sixty-something e-mails across seven drips, and before sending each one i asked myself: would i open this? the numbers say people did. take the free cloud drip. it went to the hardest crowd, people who hadn't decided yet if they even cared about Plane. twenty-four thousand people got it, a quarter of a million e-mails in all. a third of them opened the first e-mail. the fifth e-mail, the one about superpowers, did even better and got opened by thirty-four percent. that's rare, because most people stop reading a drip after the first couple of e-mails. over thirteen e-mails and six months, the open rate never fell below a quarter. most drips start strong and fade. this one kept people opening until the end. that's because every e-mail had something worth reading, and people notice that quickly. so that's the drip. sixty-something e-mails, and i'd send every one of them again. thanks for reading this far. you'd have opened e-mail thirteen too. 😉
